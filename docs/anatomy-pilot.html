Icon Anatomy Pilot

The working-out behind Asset Anatomy: the twelve icons in full, the measurements, and the arguments that were rejected. Start there if you want the model rather than the evidence.

Twelve product icons, chosen to break a four-part vocabulary if it is breakable. The point of the pilot is not to annotate twelve icons. It is to find out how many part names the other seventy-eight will need.

What is new in this revision. The pilot twelve are now the Customizer's default set, so every style pipeline runs against exactly the icons annotated here. The vocabulary is unchanged. Two sections are new: how a part is addressed from outside the file, and what the request to "add a stroke" turned out to mean.

Why product icons and not system icons

System icons are the larger collection and the obvious first thought, but the artwork cannot carry anatomy. 2,850 of 2,891 are a single merged path. There is no geometry to name, so any anatomy would be metadata asserted on top of a drawing that cannot support it, and nothing would be able to verify it.

Product icons are genuinely assembled: 90% have three or more shapes, and they carry gradients, masks and groups. They are also the set where anatomy pays off twice. They sit inside the roughly 210 full-colour assets Customizer can actually work on, and they map to Bridge's kind: app. System icons would have served neither: Customizer excludes them, and they already recolour through currentColor.

The twelve

All twelve are now the Customizer's default set, so a result proved here shows up there the same day. When this was first drafted only four of them were, and the Customizer carried its own copy of the artwork; it now loads its set from the library by id, and opens on this group. The twelve are chosen to span construction, not popularity, including both extremes: if a vocabulary survives GitHub at one shape and SwiftKey at thirty-six, the middle will not surprise anyone.

The proposed vocabulary

Four part names, deliberately few. Every part name added is a decision every future asset has to answer, so the pilot should be trying to prove four is too few, not looking for reasons to add a fifth.

A part is a set of shapes that share a ramp, not a contiguous region. This is the definition that makes four names sufficient, and it came out of Teams. The two head circles sit visually apart from everything else and look at first like they need a name of their own. They do not: they belong to the plate with the bodies beneath them, and the ramp confirms it, all four shapes inside one narrow band from 0.322 to 0.561. Changing the plate's hue has to move all four or the figures come apart. Whether shapes touch is irrelevant; whether they recolour together is the whole test.

plate

The foundation

What everything else sits on and takes its colour from. Word's document, Teams' rounded square, Microsoft's four tiles. Usually several shapes deep, and the part that carries the tonal ramp.

glyph

The mark itself

The letterform or symbol that identifies the product. The W, the X, the Copilot ribbon. What survives if you throw everything else away.

badge

A secondary mark

Something applied on top that modifies meaning rather than carrying it: the taskbar magnifier, a status dot, an overlay.

accent

Depth and articulation

Folds, highlights, shadow plates, the page edge on OneNote. Present for form, not for meaning. The part most likely to be dropped at 16px.

Why glyph and not mark. Both words have been used in conversation and they cannot both stay. Mark is already doing a job above: a bare mark is a whole icon that is nothing but its symbol, which is what Copilot and Edge are. If the part were also called a mark, "a bare mark is a single mark" is the sentence we would be left with. glyph is also the more precise word for a letterform, which is what this part is in nine of the twelve.

Why plate and not container. Bridge already has a role called container: its schemas/ directory holds exactly four, container, control, element and page. Reusing it here would give one word two meanings across two systems that are meant to interoperate, which is the same collision this library has already had to unpick twice. plate is not a coinage either: Bridge uses it 135 times already (backplate 63, stackedplate 46, plate 24), and it is the word the icon work has been using in conversation all along. base was the other candidate, but it is overloaded in every token vocabulary going.

One measurement already supports this. Every plate-and-letterform icon in the library has exactly one white fill, and in every case it is the letterform. Word, Excel, PowerPoint, OneNote, Visio, SharePoint, Teams, Outlook and Power BI all show white × 1. That means glyph is not purely an authoring judgement for this family; it is partly detectable, which is what makes annotating the other seventy-eight tractable rather than a week of clicking.

The second layer, and why it is smaller than it looks

A plate is not one shape. Word's is built from several at different depths, and a recolour that flattens them loses the form. So the obvious move is a second layer of anatomy naming those depths. The measurement argues against it.

Here is every painted shape in Word, by luminance:

Word        0.097  0.269  0.285  0.499  0.571  0.589  0.730  0.789  1.000
Excel       0.198  0.367  0.380  0.484  0.619  0.641  0.693  0.744  0.826  0.848  1.000
OneNote     0.187  0.272  0.345  0.408  0.661  0.681  1.000
Outlook     0.187  0.247  0.266  0.353  0.359  0.383  0.446  0.631 ... 1.000

Nine shapes, nine distinct values. This is a continuous ramp, not three or four named depths, and the steps are not evenly spaced. That uneven spacing is the depth cue. Naming steps would quantise it and throw the cue away.

Tone is derivable. Membership is not. Any tool can read that a shape sits at 0.269. What no tool can read is that shapes 0, 3 and 6 are all the plate and therefore belong to one ramp. That is exactly what data-part supplies, and once the grouping is known, "map this part's existing ramp onto the new hue, preserving relative spacing" is computable. Storing the tone as well would be recording a number the file already contains, and it would go stale the moment the artwork is re-exported.

One detail supports this reading: Teams has thirteen shapes but two repeated tonal pairs, 0.798 / 0.701 appearing twice. Tone is behaving as a property of role rather than of individual shape.

What luminance cannot tell you

Only one thing, but it matters. Luminance says a shape is dark. It does not say why. Word's 0.097 shape either wants to become dark green under a green recolour, or it wants to stay near-neutral because it is a shadow rather than a colour. That is a judgement. So the second layer records exceptions, not depths:

ramp

The default

Follows its part's hue, at its measured position in the ramp. Never annotated, because it is what happens when nothing is said.

shade

Stays neutral

Shadows, folds and contact shadows that read as light rather than as colour. Follows lightness, not hue.

hold

Keeps its literal colour

Brand-locked and semantic shapes. The M365 logo tiles, a warning orange. Already proven necessary by the illustration work.

Because ramp is the default, most shapes carry no tone annotation at all. The annotation cost stays close to what it was with one layer.

Where anatomy has to live

This is the one thing I would not compromise on. Anatomy belongs in the SVG, as an attribute on each shape, and not in meta.json as an index into the SVG.

// Breaks silently. Shape order is not stable.
"anatomy": { "plate": { "shapes": [0, 3, 6] } }
<!-- Travels with the artwork, survives a re-render. -->
<path data-part="plate" d="M2 4C2 2.9..." fill="url(#g0)"/>
<path data-part="plate" data-tone="shade" d="M9 4v7..." fill="url(#g3)"/>
<path data-part="glyph" d="M12 18h4l2..." fill="white"/>

// Two shapes, one part, one ramp. Only the exception is annotated.

We already have the evidence for this. Today's product import re-rendered all 90 icons from Figma, and shape order is decided by the exporter, not by us. An anatomy that says "shapes 4 and 5 are the glyph" is correct until the next import and wrong afterwards, with nothing failing loudly enough to notice. The same lesson the Figma node ids taught: reference things by identity, never by position.

Addressing a part, and what “add a stroke” means

Naming the parts is half of it. The other half is being able to point at one from outside the file, which is what makes anatomy a feature rather than a filing system. The address is the asset id with the part appended, and each address can carry a fill, a stroke or an effect:

windows.producticon.edge.plate     fill · stroke · effect
windows.producticon.word.glyph     fill · stroke · effect
windows.producticon.teams.plate    fill · stroke · effect

// One address per part, not per shape. The plate is four shapes in Teams
// and the address moves all four, which is the point of a shared ramp.

The stroke question was the wrong way round. The review asked whether we could target an icon and modify its stroke. These icons have no strokes. Every one of the twelve is filled shapes and nothing else, which is why the question had no target and the honest first answer was no. What is actually wanted is the ability to author a stroke: put a two pixel outline on the plate of every product icon and leave the glyph alone. That is a different feature, and it is precisely the one this document unlocks, because a stroke has to be applied to something that has a name.

This has been proved on real artwork rather than argued. Marking the shapes of one part with data-part and stroking that selection produces a clean outline on the plate with the letterform untouched, at every size, with no change to the source drawing. Without part names the same instruction has to guess at a selection by luminance, which is what makes the current pipelines lose a checkmark or flatten a subject into a blob.

One dependency worth naming. The address inherits whatever the library’s naming grammar decision produces: 2,665 of 3,129 ids carry a hyphen inside a name segment, which Bridge’s grammar does not allow. Parts sit at the end of that address, so they inherit the answer. It is one more reason to settle the grammar before annotation starts rather than after.

What it unlocks before Builder exists

Builder is the reason to do this, but it is not the first payoff, and Builder is an illustration tool rather than an icon one. Customizer gets better immediately, and it is now pointed at exactly these twelve, so the improvement is measurable on the same artwork this document annotates.

Right now its pipelines infer structure by bucketing shapes on luminance, because that is the only signal available. That inference is what makes Shield lose its checkmark and Data flatten into a blob: one threshold has to serve both "this light shape is a detail" and "this light shape is the subject", and it cannot. With parts declared, "recolour the plate, leave the glyph, drop the accent at small sizes" becomes an instruction rather than a guess.

For Bridge, the payoff is that anatomy is already its vocabulary. Its icon schema carries an anatomy block of named parts today. Matching that shape now is cheaper than inventing a second one and reconciling later, which is the same argument as the naming grammar.

What the session needs to decide

  1. Is four enough?

    Teams already tested it and four held, once a part was defined as a shared ramp rather than a region. Outlook is the remaining candidate: an envelope that is arguably plate and glyph at once. If it needs a fifth name, better to find out on twelve than on ninety.

  2. Is a shape always exactly one part?

    Gradients and masks mean a single visual element is sometimes several paths. Does data-part go on each, or does a wrapping group own it?

  3. What happens to the degenerate case?

    GitHub is one merged path carrying plate and glyph together. Is it glyph, is it unannotated, or does the vocabulary need a name for "indivisible"?

  4. Is shade a judgement or a measurement?

    If a shadow is reliably the darkest shape in its part, it may be detectable rather than authored. Worth testing on the twelve before committing anyone to annotating it by hand.

  5. Does anatomy vary by size?

    These are redrawn per size, not scaled. The 16px Word may legitimately have fewer parts than the 48px. Is anatomy per asset, or per drawing?

  6. Who owns it when Figma re-exports?

    Annotations added by hand will be overwritten by the next import unless the importer preserves them or the parts are declared upstream in Figma. This needs an answer before annotation starts, not after.
